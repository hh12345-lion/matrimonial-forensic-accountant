Understanding a party's income can be an important part of financial remedy proceedings in England and Wales. Where an individual is a company director, business owner or senior employee, their financial circumstances may involve more than a fixed annual salary. Bonuses, dividends, benefits, pension contributions and other forms of remuneration may also need to be considered.

These income streams can make it more difficult to understand how an individual is remunerated and how their income has changed over time. Payments may vary between financial years, and the timing of a payment may not necessarily correspond to the period in which the underlying performance occurred.

A matrimonial forensic accountant might be instructed to examine the available financial records, analyse the composition of remuneration and explain relevant patterns or differences. The scope of the work depends on the questions the expert has been asked to address, the records available and the circumstances of the case.

This article explains how forensic accountants may approach bonuses, dividends and complex director remuneration in divorce proceedings, and why the underlying financial information matters.

## Why Director Remuneration Can Be Difficult to Analyse

An employed individual may receive a regular salary, but remuneration arrangements for directors and business owners can be more varied.

Depending on the business and the individual's role, remuneration may include:

- Basic salary
- Annual or performance-related bonuses
- Dividends from company shares
- Commission or other incentive payments
- Benefits provided by an employer or company
- Employer pension contributions
- Payments recorded through a director's loan account
- Other contractual or discretionary payments

These categories do not necessarily have the same accounting, tax or financial characteristics. They should not automatically be treated as interchangeable forms of income.

For example, a bonus may relate to a particular performance period but be paid later. Dividends may depend on company profits, available distributable reserves and decisions about distributions. A director's loan account entry may record a movement between the director and the company rather than remuneration in the conventional sense.

A forensic accounting review can help distinguish between these categories and explain what the available records show.

## Reviewing the Composition of Remuneration

The first stage of an analysis may involve identifying the different components of an individual's remuneration and establishing how each has been recorded.

### Salary and Regular Employment Income

Salary records may provide a starting point for understanding an individual's regular earnings.

Relevant documents may include:

- Employment contracts or service agreements
- Payslips
- P60s
- Payroll records
- Personal tax returns
- Company accounts and payroll summaries

These records can be compared to understand the salary reported, the amounts paid and any differences between gross remuneration and net payments.

Where the figures do not reconcile, further information may be needed. Differences can arise for several reasons, including payment timing, deductions, adjustments or incomplete records. They should be investigated in context rather than treated as evidence of an unexplained payment without further analysis.

### Bonuses and Performance-Related Payments

Bonuses may form a significant part of remuneration for some directors and senior employees.

A bonus could be linked to company profitability, individual performance, contractual targets or a discretionary arrangement. The method of calculation and timing of payment may vary.

Depending on the instruction, a forensic accountant may examine:

- Bonus scheme documentation
- Employment or service agreements
- Board minutes or relevant company records
- Bonus calculations
- Payroll records
- Bank statements
- Accounting entries
- Correspondence concerning the award or payment

The purpose might be to establish the amount awarded, when it was recognised in the records, when it was paid and which period it related to.

For example, a bonus paid in one financial year may relate to performance in the preceding year. A review that considers only the payment date could therefore provide an incomplete picture of the remuneration pattern.

Where bonus payments fluctuate, the available evidence may help explain whether the variation reflects changes in performance, contractual arrangements, payment timing or other factors. The records may not always support a definitive explanation, particularly where supporting documentation is limited.

## Analysing Dividends Received by a Director or Shareholder

Dividends can be relevant where a party holds shares in a company and receives distributions from it. However, dividends differ from salary and should be analysed with regard to the company's financial records and the individual's shareholding.

### Documents Relevant to Dividend Analysis

Depending on the circumstances, the review may include:

- Dividend vouchers
- Dividend declarations
- Board minutes or resolutions
- Company accounts
- Management accounts
- Dividend payment records
- Company and personal bank statements
- Shareholding records
- Relevant tax returns

These records may help establish the amounts declared, the dates involved, the payments received and how the transactions were recorded.

Where the information differs between company records, personal bank statements and tax documentation, the differences may require further examination.

### Declared Dividends and Dividends Received

It is important to distinguish between a dividend being declared, a dividend becoming payable and the corresponding cash being received.

The dates and accounting treatment may differ. A declared dividend may not have been paid immediately, while a bank payment described as a dividend may require supporting records to establish its nature.

A forensic accountant may compare the available records to understand the sequence of events and identify any discrepancies requiring clarification.

The analysis should also take account of the individual's shareholding and the relevant company records. A company's total dividends cannot automatically be treated as income received by one shareholder.

### Dividend Patterns and Company Performance

Historical dividend records may help illustrate how distributions have changed over time.

However, dividend levels do not necessarily follow company profits in a straightforward way. Relevant considerations may include the company's financial position, available distributable reserves, cash requirements, investment plans and the decisions made about distributions.

A forensic accountant may compare dividends with company accounts and other financial information to explain the observed pattern. Whether a particular level of dividends is sustainable or likely to continue will depend on the circumstances and may require assumptions that should be clearly identified.

## Examining Benefits and Other Forms of Remuneration

Director remuneration may also include benefits or payments that do not appear as a straightforward salary or dividend.

Depending on the arrangements, these may include:

- Company cars or vehicle benefits
- Private medical insurance
- Accommodation or other benefits
- Employer pension contributions
- Commission
- Share-based remuneration
- Other contractual or discretionary payments

The relevant records may include payroll reports, benefit statements, pension documentation, employment agreements and company accounting entries.

The analysis may distinguish between cash received by the individual and benefits or contributions provided on their behalf. These categories can have different implications for understanding the individual's financial circumstances.

The treatment of a benefit for accounting, tax or legal purposes may also differ. A forensic accountant's role is to analyse the financial records within the scope of the instruction, rather than to assume that every benefit has the same significance in financial remedy proceedings.

## The Role of Company Accounts and Management Information

An analysis of director remuneration may require information about the company as well as the individual's personal records.

Company accounts and management information can provide context for understanding how remuneration has been recorded and how it relates to the business's financial performance.

### Comparing Salary, Bonuses and Dividends With Company Results

A forensic accountant may compare remuneration records with available information about:

- Turnover and profitability
- Operating costs
- Cash flow
- Retained profits
- Dividends declared and paid
- Directors' remuneration disclosed in the accounts
- Changes in the company's financial position

These comparisons may help explain whether remuneration changed alongside business performance or whether other factors appear relevant.

However, a comparison alone does not establish why a payment was made or whether a particular remuneration decision was appropriate. Further records may be required to understand the circumstances.

### Accounting Profit and Available Cash

Accounting profit and cash available to a company are not necessarily the same.

A company may report a profit while having substantial working capital requirements, debt repayments or planned expenditure. Conversely, cash balances may reflect earlier trading periods, borrowing or the timing of receipts and payments.

For this reason, reported profits should not automatically be equated with the amount available for remuneration or distribution. The company's wider financial position may need to be considered alongside the remuneration records.

## Director's Loan Accounts and Other Payments

Not every payment between a company and its director represents salary, a bonus or a dividend.

A director's loan account may record money introduced into or withdrawn from a company, expenses paid on behalf of the company or other transactions between the parties. Its treatment depends on the underlying entries and circumstances.

Where these transactions are relevant to the instruction, a forensic accountant may examine:

- Director's loan account ledgers
- Supporting accounting entries
- Bank statements
- Expense records
- Repayment documentation
- Relevant company records
- Correspondence explaining particular transactions

The aim may be to understand the nature and timing of the transactions and how they have been recorded.

A debit or credit balance should not be interpreted in isolation. The accounting balance may require reconciliation with the underlying transactions, and its significance depends on the facts of the case.

## Analysing Remuneration Over Time

A single year's remuneration may not fully explain an individual's financial circumstances, particularly where income is variable.

Depending on the scope of the instruction, a forensic accountant may examine several financial years to identify patterns and changes.

The analysis may consider:

- Changes in basic salary
- The frequency and size of bonus payments
- Dividend levels and timing
- Changes in benefits or pension contributions
- Variations in company performance
- Differences between accounting periods and payment dates
- One-off or exceptional payments
- Missing or inconsistent records

Historical information can help place current remuneration in context. However, past payments do not necessarily establish what an individual will receive in the future.

Where remuneration has changed significantly, the reasons may require further evidence. The available records might show a change in payment levels without establishing the underlying cause.

Any interpretation should therefore distinguish between documented facts, reasonable inferences and matters that remain uncertain.

## How Remuneration Analysis May Assist Financial Remedy Proceedings

The relevance of remuneration analysis depends on the issues in dispute and the expert's instructions.

For example, the analysis may assist with understanding:

- The composition of an individual's reported income
- Differences between salary, bonuses and dividends
- The timing of remuneration payments
- The relationship between company records and personal receipts
- Changes in remuneration over time
- Transactions requiring further clarification
- The financial information available for consideration by the parties and their legal advisers

The analysis may also identify limitations in the available information or areas where further documents are needed.

A forensic accountant does not determine the outcome of financial remedy proceedings. Questions about the legal treatment of income, the parties' needs, the treatment of business interests and the appropriate financial arrangements remain matters for the court, informed by the evidence and legal submissions.

The expert's role is to provide independent financial analysis within the scope of the instruction.

## What Documents Might Be Needed?

The documents required will depend on the remuneration arrangements and the questions being investigated.

A focused document request may include:

- Employment contracts or service agreements
- Payslips and payroll records
- Bonus scheme documentation and calculations
- Dividend vouchers and declarations
- Personal and company bank statements
- Company accounts and management accounts
- General ledger extracts
- Relevant tax returns and tax calculations
- Shareholding records
- Pension and benefits documentation
- Director's loan account records, where relevant
- Supporting correspondence or company records

Not every document will be necessary in every case. The appropriate scope should reflect the issues under consideration and the period relevant to the analysis.

Where records are missing, it may be useful to identify what is unavailable and whether alternative information exists. Any limitations arising from incomplete records should be made clear when interpreting the findings.

## Instructing a Matrimonial Forensic Accountant

Where remuneration is complex or disputed, the questions put to the expert should be clearly defined. The instruction may need to distinguish between establishing what was paid, understanding how payments were recorded and analysing changes in remuneration over time.

It might also be helpful to identify the relevant financial period, the documents already available and any specific discrepancies requiring examination.

The scope of the work will depend on the facts of the matter, the issues in dispute and the expert's agreed role in the proceedings.

## Conclusion

Bonuses, dividends and other forms of director remuneration can make income analysis more involved than a review of basic salary alone. The timing of payments, the distinction between different remuneration categories and the relationship between personal receipts and company records may all be relevant.

A matrimonial forensic accountant may examine the available documentation to explain how remuneration has been recorded, identify patterns and investigate discrepancies within the scope of the instruction. The conclusions that can be reached will depend on the quality and completeness of the records and the specific questions being addressed.

Careful analysis should distinguish between established financial information, matters that require clarification and issues that fall outside the expert's remit.
